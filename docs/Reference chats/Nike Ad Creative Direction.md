# THE ORDER QUESTION: Style-First vs Character-First

Both paths reach the finish line. They fail differently.

## Path A — Your original order (Style-First)

```
MJ style exploration → lock anchor style → 
  generate all 16 shots in that style → 
  add character consistency in NBP → 
  animate
```

**Where it shines:** You see your "world" early. Within day 1 you have a frame that screams *the look*. Motivating, sellable to stakeholders, easy to align a team around. MJ's `--sref` and moodboards are the strongest pure-style transfer tools that exist — when you're chasing a specific cinematic register, MJ-first gets you there faster than any other route.

**Where it breaks:** The runner is a recognizable human in 14 of 16 shots. When you generate the Paris wide (shot 15) in your locked style, the man in that frame is *not* your runner. He's a stylistically-consistent stranger. Now you go to NBP and try to swap your runner's face *into* a frame whose lighting, lens, grade, and skin rendering were all baked by MJ. NBP will fight you. You'll get one of three failure modes:

1. The face locks but the skin tone, hair texture, and clothing fabric subtly shift across shots → audience reads "different actor in each scene" without being able to articulate why
2. The face locks but the *style* degrades around it (NBP softens MJ's signature look in the area it's editing)
3. Both lock but it takes 30+ generations per shot to get there → budget blown by shot 6

This is the failure I've watched real teams hit on real campaigns. It's not theoretical.

## Path B — Character-First (what I'd push for)

```
NBP character sheet (4 angles, neutral lighting) → 
  lock 1 hero frame with character + style fused → 
  propagate that anchor across all 16 shots → 
  animate
```

**Where it shines:** Your runner is *the* fixed point. Every subsequent generation references a face, a build, a wardrobe that the model has already converged on. When you bring style into the mix at the hero-frame stage (shot 14 — the close-up — is the right one to lock first because it has both face and grade visible), you fuse character + style in a single anchor. From there NBP propagates with way fewer fights, because identity is the strongest constraint and you established it first.

**Where it breaks:** It's slower at the start. You spend 2-3 days on a character sheet before you've seen the "world." Less dopamine, harder to show stakeholders early. You also lose some of MJ's stylistic edge — NBP is more photographic and grounded by default; you have to work harder to push it into a signature look.

## The hybrid I actually recommend (Path C)

Neither pure path. The Style Bible's Linear Process points here:

```
PHASE 1: MJ style exploration (low-stakes, no character)
  → lock the visual DNA via 8-12 wide/establishing experiments
  → output: 1 "world anchor" frame (no human, or human as silhouette)

PHASE 2: NBP character sheet  
  → 4-angle turnaround, neutral lighting, neutral background
  → output: locked character reference pack

PHASE 3: Fuse — generate the HERO FRAME in NBP
  → input: world anchor (style ref) + character pack (identity ref)
  → this becomes Shot 14 (the close-up) — your master anchor
  → output: 1 frame that locks both character and world

PHASE 4: Propagate all 16 shots in NBP from the master anchor
```

This gives you MJ's stylistic ceiling AND character consistency, in the right order. It costs you maybe 1 extra day vs Path A but kills the rework cycle.

**My recommendation: Path C.** But Path A is defensible if you accept the rework risk.

# Research findings (with proof)

Six rules emerged consistently across Google DeepMind's official guide, Google Cloud's NBP guide, the prompting.systems consistency guide, and Leonardo.ai's prompt guide. Each one matters.

## Rule 1 — Cap references at 6, not 14
NBP supports up to 14 reference images, but **Nano-Banana Pro supports up to 14 reference images (6 with high fidelity)**, and **a practical standard is to cap your uploads at 6 high-quality references to maintain precise structural accuracy**. More than 6 actually *degrades* output. Counterintuitive but proven.

## Rule 2 — Number your references and assign each a job
**To get the best results, use the Multi-Image Reference feature. Label your face reference as Image 1 and your target scene as Image 2.** This is non-negotiable. Don't say "the face image" — say "Image 1." NBP parses these as anchored slots.

## Rule 3 — State what STAYS, then what CHANGES
**Change background / scene: state what stays fixed (face, pose, clothing, composition), then describe the new environment** The order matters. Preservation first, transformation second. Reverse the order and you get drift.

## Rule 4 — Explicitly request lighting match
**Ensure the new object matches the lighting and perspective of the original image.** NBP has a 3D-aware engine but you have to *invoke* it. Without "match the lighting" it'll do a flat composite.

## Rule 5 — Identity locking has a specific phrase
**Identity Locking: Explicitly state: "Keep the person's facial features exactly the same as Image 1."** This exact construction is the proven anchor phrase. Don't paraphrase it.

## Rule 6 — Chain edits, don't bundle them
**For large edits, break work into a chain of smaller edits rather than one massive instruction. Example chain: (1) background swap → (2) outfit update → (3) color grading → (4) final retouch.** Bundling all your asks into one prompt fails. Two passes is better than one.
