- **Build a 360° character sheet first.** Generate 2–3 reference images showing the character from different angles (frontal, 45°, profile). Then use these as references for new scenes.

## What Kerala Christians actually wore in the 1920s

The clothing details are crucial for period accuracy, and **Syrian Christian and Latin Catholic dress differed significantly** from each other and from the rest of Kerala's population.

### Syrian Christian women: the chatta-mundu

The signature garment was the **chatta** — a loose, jacket-like cotton top (not a modern fitted blouse) with a **V-shaped neckline** in front, semi-circular at the back, with **long sleeves** reaching the wrist or near the elbow. It was always **spotlessly white** plain cotton, reaching to the waist. Before Portuguese contact, this garment is believed to have Judean/Jewish origins, similar to garments worn by Cochin Jewish women.

The lower garment was a **white cotton mundu**, approximately 7 yards long, wrapped around the waist to the ankles. Its most distinctive feature was the **njori** — an artistically pleated fan-shaped arrangement of fabric at the back hip, created by folding multiple pleats and fringes. This fan-like formation is the unmistakable hallmark of a Syrian Christian woman's draping style.

Over the chatta, women draped a **kavani** — a creamish-white rectangular cloth with a **gold kasavu border** — over the left shoulder, tucked into the mundu at the waist, and pinned to the blouse with a **golden brooch**. In church, a separate white cloth with gold border was worn **over the head**, covering hair and shoulders.

### The jewelry tells the story of identity

The most distinctive earring was the **mekka mothiram** — large, moon-shaped gold hoop earrings of **Hebrew/Jewish origin**, worn exclusively by Syrian Christian and Cochin Jewish women, never by Hindu women. These are almost certainly the large hoop earrings visible in old reference photos. **Jimikki** (bell-shaped jhumka earrings with filigree work) were also popular.

Essential necklaces included the **minnu** — the Christian marriage pendant, a heart-shaped gold medallion with a cross formed by **13 golden beads**, hung on a thread made of 21 strands. The **elakkathali** was a choker necklace with tiny gold leaflets that shimmered with movement. The **manga mala** featured mango-shaped gold pendants, often with colored glass stones. Gold bangles (**thadavala** for thick kada-style, **ottavala** for thin) were worn from wrist toward elbow.

For a married woman's portrait, the essential visible pieces would be: mekka mothiram hoop earrings, minnu on chain, elakkathali choker, one additional necklace (manga mala), gold bangles, and the golden brooch pinning the kavani.

### Hair and styling

Women wore the **Malayala kondai** — hair oiled generously with coconut oil, **center-parted**, pulled back tightly into a smooth round **low bun** at the nape. The glossy, jet-black hair was a point of pride. Fresh **white jasmine flowers (mulla)** were often tucked into or around the bun. With hair pulled back, the elongated earlobes bearing heavy mekka mothiram earrings would be prominently visible.

### Syrian Christian men in the 1920s

A critical timeline detail: **shirts only became popular around 1930**. In the first part of the 20th century, men typically wore a **white cotton mundu** (possibly with gold kasavu border) with the upper body either bare or draped with a **thollmundu/melmundu** — a cloth shawl over the shoulders. For formal portraits, a **jubba** (long, flowing kurta-like cotton or silk shirt) was worn. Some educated men wore a **coat** in English style, but not pants. Men also tied a **folded white cloth on their head** — distinctive to the community. A visible **gold cross pendant** marked Christian identity. Simple sandals were worn as footwear.

### Latin Catholic women differed notably

Latin Catholics, largely later converts from fishing communities under Portuguese missionary influence, dressed with **more European elements** — fitted blouses with slight collars or ruffles, colored or darker-shaded sarees rather than exclusively white, and sometimes pleated skirts with loose tops. Their jewelry tended toward jimikki rather than mekka mothiram, and was generally simpler.

## Achieving the authentic 1920s photograph look

The dominant photographic process in 1920s India was the **gelatin silver print** — this is the single most important keyword for period-accurate AI generation. Studio portraits used **large format cameras** (4×5 or 8×10 glass plate negatives) with lenses typically at f/4.5, producing extremely high-resolution images with characteristic shallow depth of field.

Kerala studios of this era relied on **natural daylight through glass roofs or large windows**, creating soft, directional light with deep shadows — often a Rembrandt lighting pattern. **Negative retouching was universal**: a "pink wash" tint was applied to lighten skin tones, and facial features were sharpened with pencil work on negatives. Portraits were formal, stiff, and dignified — subjects almost never smiled.

The optimal prompt architecture follows this sequence: **photographic process → era → subject description → clothing → setting → lighting → camera/technical → aging effects → mood**. A complete example:

> *"Gelatin silver print, 1920s formal studio portrait of a young Kerala Syrian Christian woman, dark complexion, large dark eyes, center-parted oiled black hair in a smooth low bun with jasmine flowers, wearing a white cotton chatta with long sleeves and V-neckline, white mundu with njori pleats at back hip, white kavani with gold kasavu border draped over left shoulder pinned with golden brooch, large gold mekka mothiram moon-shaped hoop earrings, gold elakkathali choker necklace, minnu pendant with cross, gold bangles, standing on the deep verandah of a Kerala tharavadu with carved wooden pillars and laterite stone walls, natural window light from the left creating Rembrandt lighting, high contrast, fine analog film grain, subtle vignette, shallow depth of field with soft focus edges, aged sepia-toned print with minor foxing, formal dignified expression"*

For negative prompts (Stable Diffusion workflows): exclude modern elements, digital artifacts, HDR, smooth airbrushed skin, color photography, contemporary clothing, flash photography, ring light reflections, and any modern lighting signatures.

## Placing characters in front of consistent Kerala house backgrounds

The most reliable method uses a **layered compositing approach**. First, generate the background independently — a Kerala **nalukettu/tharavadu** with its steeply sloped clay tile roof, deep verandah, carved wooden pillars with ornamental capitals, laterite stone walls, and lime-washed surfaces. Syrian Christian homes (called **meda** or **veedu**) had distinctive features: a granary at the entrance, a small cross above the door, and by the 1920s, some Western-influenced additions like separate living rooms and arched windows.

Three approaches work, in order of quality:

**Inpainting with a fixed background** produces the most consistent results. Generate the background first, mask the character area, then inpaint the character into the scene. Use staged denoising — composition fix at 0.7–0.9, subject refinement at 0.5–0.7, detail enhancement at 0.3–0.5, cleanup at 0.2–0.4. **Lighting direction consistency** between character and background is the biggest giveaway of compositing — match both carefully.

**ComfyUI LayerForge** offers Photoshop-style multi-layer editing directly in the generation pipeline, supporting blend modes, masking, and transformations connected to FLUX inpainting nodes. This combines with ControlNet for structural guidance and IP-Adapter for style consistency.

**Nano Banana Pro's conversational editing** is simplest: upload both background image and character reference, then prompt naturally — "Place this character standing on this verandah, matching the lighting direction and vintage aesthetic of the background." Then iterate: "Move her slightly to the left," "Add more shadow on the right side of her face to match the window light direction."

## Conclusion

The most practical pipeline for this specific project — recreating 1920s Kerala Christian portraits from older reference photos — is a three-stage process. **Stage one: restore and extract.** Clean up the reference photo using CodeFormer or Topaz Photo AI, then extract the face at maximum resolution. **Stage two: de-age and generate.** Use Nano Banana Pro for initial de-aging with conversational refinement, or InstantID + IP-Adapter FaceID Plus V2 in ComfyUI for maximum face fidelity. Lock bone structure, eye shape, nose, and ears while reducing age markers. **Stage three: compose the final portrait.** Generate the Kerala tharavadu background separately, composite the character using inpainting with matched Rembrandt-style window lighting, dress in historically accurate white chatta-mundu with njori pleats and gold mekka mothiram earrings (for women) or white mundu with thollmundu shawl (for men), and apply gelatin silver print aging effects — fine grain, sepia toning, vignetting, subtle foxing.

The single biggest factor in success isn't the prompt — it's the **quality and quantity of reference images**. Multiple clear, well-lit photos of the person from different angles at 1024px+ resolution will outperform any prompting technique applied to a single degraded snapshot.
